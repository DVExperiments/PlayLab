How it works

  1. Read the brief and look at the training data: how many of each kind of apple the model learns from.
  2. Train the model and test it. Check the results for each kind of apple, not just the overall score.
  3. Change the data or what the model can see, then train again.
  4. Try to get every kind of apple to 85% or better, then reflect on where this happens in real life.

The brief

A fruit co-op in Hawke’s Bay wants an app that sorts ripe apples from unripe ones. It grows red, yellow and green varieties. For each apple, the app knows two things: its colour, and its sweetness from a handheld sugar sensor.

You’ll train a simple model by showing it labelled examples. The catch: most of the photos you’ve been given came from one orchard that grows red apples.

    Where this happens for real

    • Faces. Studies such as Gender Shades (2018) found commercial face analysis tools made far more mistakes for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men, partly because of who was in the training data.
    • Voices. Speech recognition often works less well for accents and languages that are under-represented in its training recordings.
    • Health. Tools trained on data from one population can be less accurate for others, such as a skin-condition tool trained mostly on lighter skin.
    • In Aotearoa. Te Hiku Media built te reo Māori speech recognition using recordings from its own communities, and kept control over how that data is used.
